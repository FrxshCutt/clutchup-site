Why people fail the UK driving test

Around half of attempts end in a fail — and almost never because of anything dramatic. The list of reasons is short, repetitive, and very fixable.

Failing the driving test feels like a personal disaster, so it's worth saying plainly: it is completely ordinary. The national car pass rate was 50.0% in the year to March 2026 (DVSA table DRT121A) — a coin flip, before you adjust for where you take the test. Half of everyone who walks into a test centre walks out without a pass certificate, and most of them are perfectly competent learners who will pass next time.

The second thing worth saying is that failing is usually boring. People imagine fails come from mounting a kerb during a manoeuvre or pulling out in front of a bus. In reality, the DVSA publishes the faults recorded most often every year, and the list is dominated by routine, trainable habits: not looking properly at a junction, missing a mirror check, drifting over road markings. The same entries top the table year after year. If you know what they are — and train against them deliberately — you're preparing for the test people actually sit, not the one they imagine.

How the marking actually works

Examiners record three kinds of fault. A driver fault (a "minor") is a mistake that doesn't cause immediate risk — a slightly late mirror check, a clunky gear change. A serious fault is something potentially dangerous, or a recurring minor that shows a habit rather than a slip. A dangerous fault involves actual danger to you, the examiner, or other road users. You fail if you record sixteen or more driver faults, or a single serious or dangerous fault.

That structure explains most fails. Very few people rack up sixteen minors — most fails come from one serious fault, and most serious faults are ordinary mistakes made at the wrong moment: the late look that happened to be at a busy junction, the missed mirror check that happened to matter. Our guide to what happens on test day covers the format itself; this one is about what actually goes wrong during it.

The ten faults recorded most often

These are the ten faults most often recorded on car driving tests between April 2025 and March 2026, in order, from DVSA published statistics (table DRT121F):

2Mirrors — change directionTurning, changing lane or overtaking without checking mirrors first
3Junctions — turning rightPoor position, cutting the corner, or misjudging oncoming traffic
4Response to signs — traffic lightsGoing through late ambers, stopping past the line, missing filter arrows
5Control — steeringDrifting wide, clipping kerbs, jerky corrections

This list barely moves. The 2024–25 table from the same source contains the identical ten entries in a slightly different order — and junction observation and mirror checks were first and second in both years. Whatever changes about cars, roads and learners, the ways people fail stay the same.

What the list is really telling you

Observation at junctions (faults 1 and 3)

The single most common fault in the country is not looking properly before emerging from a junction — and turning right, the hardest junction task, is third. Under test pressure, learners "look" without seeing: the head turns, but the brain is busy managing the clutch, the examiner and the nerves, so the look doesn't register an approaching car or cyclist. The fix is to make observation a deliberate act, not a gesture. In lessons and private practice, slow the approach down until there's time for a full look each way — and a second look before committing. Speed of approach is the hidden cause here: arrive too fast and there is simply no time to observe properly, whatever your intentions.

Mirror routines (faults 2 and 6)

Mirror checks before changing direction, and full checks before moving off, are pure habit — and that's exactly why they fail people. A habit either fires automatically or it doesn't; under pressure, nobody remembers to do something they haven't automated. The routine has to be drilled until it's boring: mirrors before every signal, signal before every manoeuvre, blind spot before every move-off, on every drive, including the five-minute trip to the shop. The difficulty for families practising privately is that a supervising parent usually can't tell whether the checks actually happened — which is why this fault survives so many hours of practice untouched.

Signs, lights and markings (faults 4, 7 and 8)

Three of the top ten are versions of the same problem: not responding to the information painted on and hung over the road. It's rarely ignorance — learners know what a red light means. It's tunnel vision. When your mental capacity is consumed by controlling the car, scanning shrinks to the ten metres in front of the bonnet, and the speed-limit change or lane arrow never gets seen. The cure is volume and variety: enough hours that car control becomes automatic and frees attention for the road, and enough unfamiliar routes that reading signs stays an active skill rather than route memory. Commentary driving — saying aloud what you see ahead — is an old instructor trick that works.

Control and positioning (faults 5 and 9)

Steering faults and poor road position are usually the same root cause: looking too close to the car. Steer at the tarmac ten metres ahead and you'll weave, drift wide on bends and hug parked cars; look well down the road and the car straightens itself out. Smoothness is trainable — wide quiet roads first, then progressively tighter streets — and it improves fastest when you get honest feedback about when the wobbles actually happen, because most learners can't feel them from the driving seat.

Progress (fault 10)

The one that surprises parents: you can fail for driving too slowly. Crawling at 20 in a clear 30, or sitting at an empty roundabout waiting for permission that never comes, is marked as failing to maintain progress — because hesitant driving forces other people to react to you. It's a confidence fault, not a skill fault, and confidence comes almost entirely from time behind the wheel. If hesitancy is your pattern, the answer is rarely more theory — it's more miles, as our guide to how many lessons you actually need argues in more detail.

The uncomfortable truth: these are habits

Look back down that list. Almost nothing on it is a knowledge gap or a freak event. Mirror checks, approach speed, steering smoothness, keeping to the limit, holding your lane — these are habits, built by repetition. And that cuts both ways: private practice is the cheapest way to build good habits, and the easiest way to rehearse bad ones. A learner who spends twenty unsupervised-in-all-but-name hours turning without mirror checks hasn't practised for the test — they've practised failing it, thoroughly.

The short version

  • Half of attempts fail, and mostly for routine faults — junction observation and mirror checks top the DVSA's list year after year.
  • One serious fault is enough to fail, and most serious faults are ordinary mistakes made at a bad moment.
  • Nearly everything on the top-ten list is a habit: drill mirrors, approach speed and observation until they're automatic, not remembered.
  • Private practice rehearses whatever you already do — good or bad — so make sure it comes with honest feedback.
